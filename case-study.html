The scenario

A small team with a model and no wet lab

A computational biology team uses a generative model to design protein binders against PD-L1, a well-known immune target. The model proposes thousands of candidates. They shortlist 96.

Their problem is the next step. Setting up expression and binding assays in-house means buying equipment, hiring a technician and waiting weeks per batch. Until they have measurements, they can't tell which designs work, and the model can't learn from its misses.

Scope

What you bring, what we do

The line is simple. We start at the sequence and end at the data.

We do

  • Convert your sequences to DNA and express each protein
  • Measure expression yield for every design
  • Run binding assays against your target, with replicates and controls
  • Measure thermostability for each protein
  • Return clean, labeled, machine-readable results

We don't

  • Design the proteins for you
  • Decide which targets or epitopes to go after
  • Interpret the biology or run clinical work
  • Keep or reuse your sequences without agreement
The workflow

What happens after you press submit

Day 0

Submit

The team uploads a CSV of 96 sequences, picks PD-L1 as the target and selects all three assays, using the console or the API.

Input: sequences.csv
Day 1

Express

Sequences become DNA and go into automated cell-free expression. Each protein is checked for yield before it moves on.

Proteins that fail to express are flagged, not silently dropped.
Days 2–3

Measure

Robots run binding and stability assays with three replicates and control proteins on every plate.

Raw curves are kept alongside the summary numbers.
Day 3 onward

Deliver

Results appear in the console and by API: a ranked table, plots and downloadable data, ready for the next design round.

Turnaround here is a target for the concept, not a promise.

Closing the loop

Failures are data too

The team feeds all 96 measurements, including the designs that didn't express or bind, back into their model. Round 2 targets the traits shared by the strong binders.

In this simulated example the share of strong binders rises from 9 in 96 to 24 in 96. That's the point of the loop: every batch makes the next batch of designs better.

What the team walked away with

  • A ranked shortlist of binders ready for deeper testing
  • Clean training data, including negatives, for their model
  • Raw curves and controls they can audit or re-analyze
  • A repeatable way to run the next round without new infrastructure

What happens next

  • Top binders move to orthogonal validation and larger-scale production
  • The team runs round 3 on a new epitope or a harder target
  • Each cycle adds to a dataset that is theirs to keep
